# Aristotle — *Poetics*: Reference Notes

*Research notes gathered as background for character-generation and dialogue work, companion to `stanislavski-an-actor-prepares.md`. Where Stanislavski is about how a performer builds truth from the inside, Aristotle is about how a story is constructed from the outside — what makes an action follow from what came before, and what makes a character legible through what they do. A short "relevance" line under each concept connects it to what has actually gone wrong in eval on this project.*

**One caveat up front.** NoExit is structurally anti-Aristotelian by design. There is no reversal of fortune available in the present tense — everyone is already dead, nothing can change, and the whole point is that there is no exit. Aristotle's architecture applies to the **backstories**, which are compressed tragedies, and to the **shape of a session**, which is a recognition sequence. It does not apply to the room, which is deliberately static. Reading his rules as prescriptions for the play itself would break the premise.

---

## Plot and structure

### Plot before character (mythos over ethos)

Of the six parts of tragedy — plot, character, thought, diction, melody, spectacle — plot is first and character second. Aristotle is blunt: without action there cannot be a tragedy, but there may be one without character. Character is not a description of a person; it is what is revealed by the organisation of incidents. Character comes in as subsidiary to the actions.

*Relevance: the sharpest available diagnosis of what separates the strong and weak fields in our bios. The best material in the 2026-08-29 run named acts — falsifying the attendance logs, bypassing lockout-tagout to save two hours. The weakest was adjectival or purely interior — "Quietly furious," and a "refuse to admit" field made of mood. Aristotle's test: a trait no action demonstrates is not character, it is a label.*

### Character is revealed through choice

Character is what discloses moral purpose — what a person chooses or avoids when the choice is not obvious. Speech that reveals no choice reveals no character. This is why Aristotle ranks *dianoia* (thought) and *lexis* (diction) below plot: fine speech attached to no decision is empty.

*Relevance: the missing dialogue rule, stated precisely. Our characters currently have a want sitting in their bio that the dialogue prompt never instructs them to pursue, so replies express mood rather than enact a decision. It also sharpens the "utilitarian not poetic" complaint: the objection was never to good writing, it was to writing that carried no choice.*

### Necessity and probability

The governing law. Events must follow one another by necessity or probability, and — critically — Aristotle extends the same law to character: *"a person of a given character should speak or act in a given way, by the rule either of necessity or of probability, just as this event should follow that by necessary or probable sequence."* Surprise is permitted, but only if it reads afterwards as the plausible or necessary consequence of what preceded it.

*Relevance: this is the plausibility test that the last run failed twice. Linda's sin happens on a travel soccer team, which is a club, not a school — so a middle-school assistant principal holding its volunteer logs is neither necessary nor probable. Same failure as the earlier "why would the boy sing off-key at home but not in the classroom." The rule to encode is Aristotle's, applied to supporting characters as well as leads.*

### Unity of action, and the whole

A whole has a beginning, a middle and an end. A beginning does not follow from anything by causal necessity; a middle follows something and is followed by something; an end follows but has nothing following it. The plot must be one uninterrupted causal chain with nothing superfluous — *any part that could be added or removed without great effect on the rest is superfluous and damages the unity.*

*Relevance: the removability test is a better eval instrument than "does it hang together," because it is answerable. Take any field out of a bio — the occupation, the hated person, the trait — and ask whether anything else changes. Human eval on the 28th made exactly this criticism without the vocabulary: "'who you hated' is vividly written and then completely unused in the sin." That is a superfluous part, and Aristotle says it costs the whole.*

### Magnitude

Beauty depends on magnitude and order. The action must be long enough to permit a change of fortune, and short enough to be held in the mind and memory at once.

*Relevance: the prose paragraphs in the last run ran ~170 words against a stated 80–100. Not fatal, but magnitude is a real constraint rather than a formatting preference — the bio has to be holdable in one grasp, both by a reader and by the model that will act from it.*

### The unravelling must arise from the plot itself

*"The unravelling of the plot, no less than the complication, must arise out of the plot itself; it must not be brought about by the Deus ex Machina."* External intervention may only report events outside the drama's range — antecedent or subsequent things beyond human knowledge. The resolution has to come from within.

*Relevance: this is the cause-of-death rule, stated 2,300 years early. Human eval put it as "I want to avoid deaths due to other people's faults; the deaths have to be always related to the character's choices," and the prompt now says the death must follow from something the character did, chose or avoided. Aristotle's framing is the more general principle, and it extends further than death — it argues against any bio element that arrives from outside the character's own chain of action.*

## The tragic mechanism

### Hamartia

Usually mistranslated "tragic flaw." The word means to miss the mark. The scholarly split is real and worth knowing: one reading takes it as a moral defect deserving punishment (medieval and neoclassical, tied to poetic justice), the other as a morally neutral error of judgement or ignorance — Oedipus is destroyed by not knowing who his parents are, not by a vice. Aristotle pairs it with the requirement that the protagonist be neither exceptionally virtuous nor villainous, because a wholly wicked person's fall provokes no pity and a wholly good person's fall is merely offensive.

*Relevance: a live design question rather than a rule to adopt. Our two most recent characters sit on opposite sides of it — Daniel made an error (chose work over his daughter, meaning no harm) while Linda committed a knowing act (forged records to avoid a confrontation). Aristotle prefers the former; Sartre, whose register the setup prompt explicitly invokes, is much closer to the latter. Worth deciding deliberately which one NoExit wants, because the prompt currently invites both and the anti-duplication block sorts sins into moral categories that mostly imply vice.*

### Peripeteia — reversal

A change to the opposite of what was intended: one or more self-destructive actions taken in blindness, producing results diametrically opposed to those aimed at. Aristotle calls it, with recognition, the most powerful part of a plot.

*Relevance: quietly present in the best material already, and worth making explicit. Daniel worked relentlessly to provide for Naomi and thereby lost her — the act produced the opposite of its aim, which is why that sin reads as tragic rather than merely bad. A generation rule requiring the true reason to have backfired on its own intention would raise the floor.*

### Anagnorisis — recognition

*"A change from ignorance to knowledge."* Most powerful when it coincides with reversal.

*Relevance: this is the actual engine of the game, and the one place where NoExit is fully Aristotelian. "What you refuse to admit about yourself" is a recognition pre-loaded and withheld — the drama is whether it fires. The dialogue rules currently instruct concealment (rule 6) but nothing describes the conditions under which the guard can break. Aristotle's pairing of recognition with reversal suggests what would make it land: it should arrive through the other characters' pressure, not by decision.*

### Pathos — suffering

The destructive or painful act. Aristotle holds it most tragic when it occurs between people bound by kinship or love — harm between intimates outweighs harm between strangers or enemies.

*Relevance: independent confirmation of the relational-sin-anchor change already made on 2026-08-23. The instruction that "the wound must be personal, not institutional" and must centre on the person loved or hated is Aristotle's rule about kinship, arrived at from a different direction.*

### Simple and complex plots

A simple plot proceeds without reversal or recognition. A complex plot contains one or both. Aristotle plainly prefers complex.

*Relevance: a usable eval axis for backstories. A bio that recounts a bad thing that happened, with no turn against intention and nothing the character has yet to realise, is a simple plot — coherent but inert. The good/neutral/bad judgement often turns on precisely this without naming it.*

## Character requirements

Aristotle names four aims:

- **Good** — meaning possessed of moral purpose, a person who chooses, not a person who is nice.
- **Appropriate** — traits fitting the person's station, age and role.
- **True to life** — recognisable as a real human being.
- **Consistent** — and, in the famous allowance, *"consistently inconsistent"* if the character is by nature contradictory.

*Relevance: "consistent, or consistently inconsistent" is a precise instrument for a judgement the eval guide currently makes by feel. It separates a genuine internal contradiction from an authoring error. Linda enforcing rules on everyone but herself is a consistent inconsistency and is the best thing about her. The travel-team jurisdiction is not a contradiction at all — it is a mistake. Same guide, two very different verdicts, and the distinction is the difference between a character who is complicated and a bio that is wrong.*

### Spectacle is the least artistic element

Ranked last of the six. Aristotle notes the tragic effect should be achievable from reading the plot alone, without staging.

*Relevance: the same argument as Stanislavski's Action, from the other end. Ornamental prose is spectacle — it decorates without contributing to the causal chain. The test survives the translation: if the effect disappears when you strip the texture, the texture was doing the work, and that is the wrong way round.*

### Catharsis

Tragedy arouses pity and fear and effects a catharsis of those emotions. Pity for undeserved misfortune, fear because the sufferer is like us.

*Relevance: sets the target emotion, and explains why the "neither virtuous nor villainous" balance matters mechanically rather than morally. If the player cannot recognise themselves in either character, the room produces judgement instead of unease — and unease is the point.*

---

## What is worth taking

**For character generation**, the strongest candidates are: the removability test for unity; necessity-and-probability extended explicitly to supporting characters; a requirement that the true reason backfired against its own intention (peripeteia); and "consistently inconsistent" as the standard for contradictions.

**For dialogue**, the strongest is character-revealed-through-choice — every reply should enact a decision, not report a state. It converges with Stanislavski's Units and Objectives from a different direction, which is some evidence it is the right next change.

**Deliberately not adopted:** the three unities as commonly understood, catharsis as a design target, and any requirement for resolution. NoExit's premise is that nothing resolves — the room is the punishment. Importing Aristotle's demand for an end would break the thing that makes it work.
